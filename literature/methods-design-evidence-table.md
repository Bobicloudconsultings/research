# Methods and Design Evidence Table

## Purpose

This table records the methodological and design lessons from literature identified for the redesign of the PhD project:

> **Justice Intervention in Response to Cult and Gang Violence in Southern Nigeria: Impact on Wellbeing, Trust, and Social Cohesion**

The purpose is not simply to collect references. Each source is examined for what it can contribute to the design, feasibility, theoretical integration, participant selection, methods, ethics, and interpretation of the proposed studies.

The literature is organised around five connected areas:

1. Evolutionary psychology of punishment, repair, revenge and reconciliation
2. Justice, restorative processes and psychological outcomes
3. Cult/gang violence and forensic qualitative research
4. Community conflict resolution, traditional institutions and peacebuilding in Nigeria
5. Qualitative methodology and research design

**Important:** Where the published source does not provide enough information to establish a methodological detail, this table states **"Not specified in source consulted"** rather than making an assumption.

# Cross-Study Design Lessons

## 1. Justice should not be treated as simply "punishment versus restorative justice"

The evolutionary literature indicates that responses to wrongdoing may involve several motivations at the same time, including:

- deterrence;
- compensation;
- retaliation/retribution;
- protection from future harm;
- restoration of relationships;
- restoration of shared values;
- reintegration of the offender.

The PhD should therefore avoid assuming in advance that participants will understand justice in only one of these ways.

Petersen et al. provide a framework for distinguishing the seriousness of wrongdoing from perceptions of the offender's future value as a social partner, while Fitouchi and Singh provide evidence for both compensatory and deterrent elements of retributive sentiment.

---

## 2. Reconciliation should be treated as something that can be investigated, not simply claimed

The McFarland and Majolo studies distinguish different forms of post-conflict interaction and examine whether those behaviours reduce post-conflict costs.

For the PhD, this raises an important design question:

> **What would count as evidence of reconciliation, restored relationships or reintegration in a Southern Nigerian community?**

That question can then be explored rather than assuming that "reconciliation" means the same thing to every participant.

---

## 3. The community should not be treated as a passive background

The Nigerian peacebuilding literature supports giving the community an active role in the research design.

El-Bushra et al. describe consultation with more than 1,200 people and show how local understandings of conflict were used in programme design.

The *Unbundling Peacebuilding* study provides Nigerian evidence linking community-level mediation and dialogue with outcomes including violence, insecurity, trust and social cohesion.

This supports the direction being considered for **Study 3**: community-level justice and reconciliation should be examined rather than treating the research purely as a victim/offender comparison.

---

## 4. Community leaders are a defensible participant group — but not necessarily the only group

The Nigerian literature includes research involving:

- traditional leaders;
- religious leaders;
- women leaders;
- youth;
- community-development professionals;
- civil-society actors;
- ordinary community members.

This provides methodological support for considering community leaders as an important participant group, but it does not by itself determine the final Study 3 sample.

The final participant groups should follow the research question, ethics assessment, feasibility and supervisory decision.

---

## 5. A higher-CGV versus lower-CGV community comparison has methodological precedent

The *Unbundling Peacebuilding* research compares communities and measures outcomes including violence, perceived security, trust, social interaction and social cohesion.

This does **not** establish that the same sampling design should be used in Southern Nigeria.

It does, however, provide a methodological precedent for asking whether **community-level exposure to violence is associated with differences in justice, trust, reconciliation or social-cohesion responses**.

---

## 6. Qualitative forensic research can provide access to experiences that quantitative measures may miss

Nnam et al. used in-depth interviews with cultists in a Nigerian custodial setting, while Bolger and Needs used IPA with former gang members to examine identity, belonging and transition out of gangs.

This supports the potential role of qualitative forensic psychology in the redesigned project.

The qualitative study should nevertheless answer a question that the evolutionary and other components cannot answer, rather than simply adding interviews to demonstrate interdisciplinarity.
